The nervous system is not another tube

Research for the nervous-system mode of My Body — Inner Voyage, answering the brief's §8 questions with real dimensions, speeds and named sources. The design that survives the evidence is a split level: Route A, the reflex arc — a cable-and-relay run from muscle spindle to quadriceps — and Route B, the blood–brain barrier and CSF — a barrier-and-drainage run from brain capillary to outflow. The action potential becomes the game's second clock, alongside the heartbeat.

Route A — the reflex arc, in seven stations

The patellar reflex is L2–L4 (predominantly L3–L4): monosynaptic for quadriceps excitation, plus a disynaptic reciprocal-inhibition branch to the hamstrings. Pick a station for its real dimensions. One scale warning from the research: the muscle spindle (millimetres) is the only station honestly visible at submarine scale — everything after it needs the zoom-station mechanic.

Where the milliseconds go

Total knee-jerk latency (tap → quadriceps EMG) in adults is of order ~15–30 ms, varying with limb length and method. The research gives a worked budget, not a single magic number — the central synapse, the part students imagine is slow, costs only ~0.5–1 ms of it.

The second clock: the action potential, drawn honestly

The AP can play the heartbeat's role — one rhythm driving the world — only if it is rendered as a regenerating wave of membrane voltage. Nothing material travels the length of the axon: ions cross the membrane locally (Na⁺ in, then K⁺ out, radially over nanometres) while local-circuit current spreads passively ahead and depolarises the next patch, which regenerates its own AP. The pattern of voltage propagates; individual ions move micrometres at most.

One spike, canonical values Established

Spike duration ~1–2 ms for a typical neuron (some course notes quote up to ~4 ms including the after-hyperpolarisation — the Codex should separate spike width from AHP). Amplitude is all-or-none and does not decay with distance; stimulus intensity is coded by frequency, not spike size. Theoretical firing ceiling ~500–1000/s; sustained physiological rates are far lower.

How to render it in-game

  • A travelling band of membrane state: colour = Vm, a live Vm(t) trace pinned to the band, Nav/Kv gate icons opening and inactivating, radial ion arrows only at the band.
  • A refractory tail that visibly cannot be re-triggered.
  • If gameplay needs a projectile, make it a camera drone synchronised to the wavefront — and say so in the Codex.
Reality check A glowing orb or bullet inside the lumen teaches the exact misconception the course will then test. Charge separation exists only across the ~5 nm membrane; bulk axoplasm stays electroneutral, and only a minuscule fraction of the Na⁺/K⁺ gradient is spent per spike.

Textbook simplification — wrong “The pump repolarises the membrane after each AP.” Repolarisation is Kv efflux; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump is housekeeping that maintains gradients over minutes.

The eight misconceptions to design against

Education literature + examining experience — pedagogy consensus, not a single-study finding. Each maps 1:1 to a rounds-quiz distractor.

  1. The AP is electrons / a charge packet travelling inside a wire-like axon.
  2. A stronger stimulus makes a bigger AP.
  3. The whole axon depolarises at once; Na⁺ rushes from soma to terminal.
  4. Myelin lets the AP “jump”, with nothing happening between nodes.
  5. Threshold is where the stimulus “pushes” the cell, not where regenerative feedback takes over.
  6. The refractory period means the cell is “tired” or out of ions.
  7. Resting potential means no ion movement (it is a leak + pump steady state).
  8. A synapse is electrical continuity.

Myelin: geometry first, then the CNS/PNS split

Saltatory conduction, worded carefully: the AP does not “jump”. Local-circuit current flows rapidly and passively through the low-capacitance, high-resistance internode and depolarises the next node to threshold, where a new, full AP is regenerated. Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance — longer length constant, faster passive spread, far less ion flux to repay. To reach 100 m/s unmyelinated would take a ~1 mm squid-like axon; that one comparison justifies myelin.

What the TEM actually shows

Compact myelin alternates the major dense line (fused cytoplasmic faces, MBP-rich) and the intraperiod line (fused extracellular faces; P0 in PNS, PLP in CNS). Paranodal glial loops form septate-like junctions (Caspr1–contactin–neurofascin-155) that fence the node and seal the periaxonal space; Kv1 channels sit sequestered at the juxtaparanodes, Kv7/KCNQ at nodes. Schmidt–Lanterman incisures and paranodal loops are retained cytoplasm, more prominent in the PNS.

Textbook simplification — wrong Perfect concentric circles, and “thicker myelin = always faster”. Real sheaths vary in wraps and carry cytoplasmic channels; past the g-ratio optimum, added myelin at fixed outer diameter shrinks the axon and slows conduction. Intermittent myelination also exists in cortex (Tomassy et al., Science, 2014).

Route B — the barrier, the fluid, and three contested exits

The flyable part of Route B is established and confident: choroid plexus → ventricles → subarachnoid space — the only true “tube world” in the nervous system. The contested part is the exit. The research's design ruling: make the exit a branching choice of three routes, with a Reality-check card stating that their shares in humans are unknown. Uncertainty becomes gameplay instead of error.

The BBB is the endothelium Established

Brain capillary endothelium is continuous and non-fenestrated, sealed by tight junctions, with very low pinocytosis/transcytosis, no intercellular gaps, high mitochondrial content, efflux transporters (P-gp/ABCB1) and a continuous basement membrane. Pericytes and astrocyte endfeet induce and maintain that phenotype — they are not the seal.

  • Tight junctions: claudin-5 dominant (knockout mice leak selectively to <~800 Da — Nitta et al., 2003), plus occludin, tricellulin, JAMs, scaffolded by ZO-1/ZO-2; VE-cadherin adherens junctions beneath.
  • Pericytes sit inside the basement membrane: ratio ~1:3 to endothelium (vs ~1:100 in skeletal muscle), coverage ~80% (quote 70–90% by region/method).
  • Astrocyte endfeet cover >90% of the abluminal surface (often >99% quoted in rodent cortex), with polarised AQP4, Kir4.1 and the dystrophin complex. Recent nuance: human endfoot AQP4 enrichment looks weaker than mouse in microvessel proteomics — don't present rodent density as measured human fact.
  • Basement membrane: two largely fused layers at capillaries (endothelial + parenchymal; collagen IV, laminin, nidogen, perlecan). Perivascular space is essentially collapsed at true capillaries — no wide moat in the art.

Textbook simplification — wrong “Astrocytes form the BBB.” Still printed; still wrong as a primary statement.

Three capillaries on one screen

  • Brain: continuous, tight-junction-sealed, no fenestrae, low transcytosis.
  • Skeletal muscle (Route A's effector): continuous yet far leakier to small solutes; elsewhere, choroid-plexus and intestinal capillaries are frankly fenestrated (~60–80 nm fenestrae with diaphragms).
  • Lymphatic (the existing detour): overlapping “button” junctions, discontinuous basement membrane, anchoring filaments — designed to leak in. The BBB is the anti-lymphatic.

The contrast reuses both existing worlds — and makes the Route B lesson tactile: the sub cannot leak out here the way it did at the muscle capillary.

Choroid plexus: the barrier moves one layer out

Plexus capillaries are fenestrated and leaky; the blood–CSF barrier sits in the choroid plexus epithelium — cuboidal, tight-junctioned, microvillous, secretory (apical Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — unusually apical — carbonic anhydrase, aquaporin-1). Plexuses sit in the lateral, third and fourth ventricles.

Textbook simplification — wrong “CSF is filtered plasma.” It is a secretion with actively set composition. The “70–80% choroid plexus, rest extrachoroidal” split is a teaching estimate — flag as approximate.

The outflow question, appraised 2024–2026

Do not ship the 2015-era diagram as settled fact. The Codex needs three columns — here they are.

  • Meningeal (dural) lymphatics exist in humans. Seen non-invasively on MRI in humans and non-human primates, confirmed histologically (PROX1⁺/LYVE1⁺/podoplanin⁺) along the superior sagittal and transverse sinuses and skull base. Absinta et al., eLife, 2017 (DOI 10.7554/eLife.29738); mouse discovery: Louveau et al., Nature, 2015 (DOI 10.1038/nature14432), Aspelund et al., J Exp Med, 2015; Jacob et al., J Exp Med, 2022.
  • New human data, 2025: dynamic contrast MRI + post-mortem mapping found a ventral drainage compartment around the middle meningeal artery with slow, non-vascular kinetics (peak ~90 min) and PROX1⁺/PDPN⁺/LYVE1⁺ structures. Promising, not definitive — the in-vivo arm was n = 5 healthy participants. Albayram et al., iScience, 2025 (DOI 10.1016/j.isci.2025.113693).
  • Perivascular CSF–interstitial exchange operates in humans: intrathecal gadolinium tracer enters human brain parenchyma along perivascular routes and clears over hours; sleep deprivation impairs tracer clearance in humans. Ringstad, Investig Radiol, 2024 (DOI 10.1097/RLI.0000000000000969, as cited in Frontiers review literature).
  • “Glymphatic” convection through parenchyma. Original model (Iliff et al., Sci Transl Med, 2012): periarterial entry, AQP4-dependent convective flow through interstitium, perivenous exit, enhanced in sleep. Challenges: transport shown diffusive and AQP4-independent in rodent parenchyma (Smith et al., eLife, 2017, DOI 10.7554/eLife.27679); diffusion rather than bulk flow in reconstructed neuropil (Holter et al., PNAS, 2017); spatial modelling against convection (Jin, Smith & Verkman, J Gen Physiol, 2016); AQP4 dependence defended by Mestre et al., eLife, 2018. Unresolved: perivascular flow is real; parenchymal bulk convection vs diffusion/dispersion is not settled.
  • Sleep increases clearance — directly challenged in 2024. Miao et al., Nat Neurosci, 2024 found reduced dye clearance in sleep/anaesthesia in mice, opposite to Xie et al., Science, 2013. Follow-on work proposes noradrenergic vasomotion in NREM as a rhythmic pump, suppressed by zolpidem. Human endpoints are almost all indirect proxies (DTI-ALPS — biologically non-specific — enlarged perivascular spaces, intrathecal contrast): fluid motion ≠ proven solute clearance. Safe in-game wording: clearance physiology is sleep-associated in several human measures; mechanism, and even the direction of the sleep effect, remain contested in animals.
  • AQP4 polarisation loss in human disease: shown in rodent TBI/Alzheimer's models, but a 2024 post-mortem veteran blast-TBI study found no polarisation change (Braun et al., 2024). No direct human in-vivo AQP4-polarisation assay exists.
  • Textbook simplification — now incomplete “CSF is absorbed through arachnoid granulations into the superior sagittal sinus” as the sole/main route. Granulations exist and conduct CSF pressure-dependently, but their quantitative share in normal humans is unknown and probably over-taught. Modern reviews list multiple parallel exits: dural lymphatics → deep cervical nodes; cribriform plate → nasal lymphatics; cranial/spinal nerve sheaths; arachnoid granulations. Proulx, Cell Mol Life Sci, 2021; NMR Biomed, 2024 (DOI 10.1002/nbm.5149).
  • Arachnoid cuff exit (ACE) points Mouse, 2024 — cuff-like arachnoid discontinuities where bridging veins cross, ringed by Prox1⁺ lymphatic-like cells, letting tracer and immune cells pass to the dura. Elegant — but human ACE anatomy is not yet established. Label: mouse-established, human-hypothesised. Smyth et al., Nature, 2024.
  • Nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus Mouse + supportive human imaging, 2024 — a major hub draining via skull-base meningeal lymphatics to deep cervical nodes; mouse ablation/ageing work attributes ~half of CSF outflow to it in mice. The “half” must never be presented as a human measurement. Yoon et al., Nature, 2024.
  • Cribriform/nasal route in humans: post-mortem tracer reaches nasal mucosa, but two human MRI studies failed to demonstrate cribriform drainage in vivo — a perfect “evidence disagrees” Codex card. Summarised in Brain Communications, 2025 (DOI 10.1093/braincomms/fcaf311).

Under the microscope: crowded, tethered, structured

Cryo-electron tomography of vitrified, unstained synapses is the ground truth for the zoom stations — and it contradicts the empty textbook bouton. The bouton is crowded, not empty: vesicles packed densest within 0–45 nm of the active zone, linked by filamentous connectors and tethers, opposite a cleft filled with visible dense material.

Inside the synaptic bouton Established from cryo-ET

  • Vesicles ~35–45 nm outer diameter (use ~40 nm in the Codex; protocol wording on radius/diameter varies).
  • Connectors link vesicle to vesicle; tethers (mostly <40 nm) link vesicles to the active zone.
  • Cleft ~20–30 nm, filled with adhesion molecules — not empty space.
  • Docked = vesicle in point contact (0 nm) with the membrane; priming needs Munc13 + SNAREs (Imig et al., Neuron, 2014).
  • Beneath primed vesicles, cryo-ET averaging finds a ring of ~6 rod-like densities, ~40 nm across — interpreted as six SNAREpins organised by Munc13. Present as a model supported by averaging, not a photograph of “the” release machine (Radhakrishnan et al., 2021).
  • Ca²⁺ channels cluster within tens of nm of docked vesicles — that distance sets release speed and probability. SNAREs themselves are below routine in-situ cryo-ET visibility; their structures come from X-ray/cryo-EM of complexes. Say so.

Fernández-Busnadiego et al., J Cell Biol, 2010 & 2013; Siegert et al., cryo-ET protocol, protocols.io, 2024.

Inside the axon: a structured gel on rails

  • Microtubules, 25 nm — long rails, plus-ends distal. Neurofilaments, 10 nm — the main determinant of axon calibre, hence of velocity. Actin, 7 nm — including periodic submembranous rings every ~190 nm (super-resolution; Xu, Zhong & Zhuang, Science, 2013), invisible in textbook diagrams and conventional EM.
  • Fast transport (kinesin out, dynein back): ~50–400 mm/day (≈1–5 µm/s instantaneous, stop-and-go). Mitochondria net ~50–100 mm/day.
  • Slow transport: component a (microtubules/neurofilaments) ~0.2–2.5 mm/day; component b (actin, soluble proteins) ~2–8 mm/day.
  • Worked numbers: soma → foot (~1 m) takes ~2–3 days by fast transport, months to over a year by slow. Peripheral regeneration runs at the famous ~1 mm/day — the same order as slow transport.

Maday et al., Neuron, 2014; Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science.

Five textbook pictures to un-teach Reality-check cards

  1. Empty axoplasm / empty bouton — false; both are densely crowded.
  2. Synapse as an empty gap with dots of transmitter — the cleft is narrow, structured and adhesion-filled; release is quantised to docked vesicles at defined active zones opposite receptor-dense densities.
  3. Myelin as passive plastic insulation — living glial membrane that clusters channels and metabolically supports the axon (lactate shuttle via MCT1 — debated in detail; established that oligodendrocyte support failure kills axons).
  4. One vesicle size, free-floating vesicles — vesicles are tethered, connector-linked and pool-organised.
  5. Golgi-stain neuron = typical neuron density — Golgi stains ~1–5% of neurons; neuropil on EM is almost entirely filled with processes.

The case decision: MS first, MG second

The brief asked which case best teaches the neuroscience students find hardest and reuses the most of the existing game. Pick a candidate to compare it against the recommendation.

The MS lesion on MRI Established

  • Ovoid T2/FLAIR lesions, ≥3 mm typical (tumefactive >2 cm exists), perpendicular to the ventricles along medullary veins — Dawson fingers; callosal, juxtacortical (U-fibre), infratentorial and cord lesions.
  • Acute lesions enhance with gadolinium for ~2–6 weeks (often open-ring) — that enhancement is BBB leak, playable on Route B.
  • Chronic T1 “black holes” = axon loss. Central vein sign and paramagnetic rim lesions (chronic active edge) are the new specific biomarkers.
  • Bedside hooks: optic neuritis, Lhermitte, Uhthoff, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, brisk reflexes + Babinski.

Neuroradiology reviews collated in Practical Neurology (2025/2026) and MAGNIMS guidance.

McDonald criteria — teach the logic, label the version 2024 revision

  • Optic nerve added as a 5th dissemination-in-space topography (periventricular, cortical/juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord, optic nerve; DIS = lesions in ≥2 of 5).
  • Kappa free light chains in CSF now accepted as equivalent to oligoclonal bands for dissemination in time.
  • Central vein sign and paramagnetic rim lesions can support diagnosis in defined scenarios; with lesions in 4–5 topographies, DIT may no longer be required; radiologically isolated syndrome can now be diagnosed as MS in defined circumstances.
  • DIT classically: simultaneous enhancing + non-enhancing lesions, a new lesion on follow-up, or CSF oligoclonal bands.

Criteria will change again, and misdiagnosis rates of 5–18% are reported when they are misapplied to mimics — so the card teaches space + time + no better explanation. 2024 revisions of the McDonald criteria, presented ECTRIMS 2024, published Lancet Neurology, 2025; prior version Thompson et al., Lancet Neurol, 2018 (DOI 10.1016/S1474-4422(17)30470-2).
