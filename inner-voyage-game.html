    You are a microscopic medical probe injected into a femoral vein. Ride one full lap of the circulation — vena cava, right heart, lungs, left heart, aorta, a working muscle and home — and work out why your patient keeps fainting.

    📋 Case 01 — Mr. Daniel Reyes, 74

    Three months of chest tightness and breathlessness when walking uphill. Last week he blacked out climbing the stairs. GP heard “a murmur”. Your job: listen at his chest first, then fly in to see why, then present on Rounds. Wrong calls show up in his clinical course.

    Help

    Flight manual

    Valves are one-way doors with timing. Tricuspid and mitral (AV) valves open in diastole, after S2. Pulmonic and aortic (semilunar) valves open in systole, right after S1. Watch the Cycle indicator and the ECG: the QRS fires just before the ventricles squeeze.

    Bedside exam (G) is the real clinical skill: you listen through the chest wall at the standard landmarks, compare spots, and call where the murmur is loudest and when it happens. Outside to hear, inside to understand.

    Inside listening (hold H) steadies the probe, quiets the engine and shows a live phonocardiogram. It is what the jet sounds like at the valve, which is not what a clinician hears; use it to understand the cause. Diaphragm favours high-pitched sounds (S1, S2, the AS murmur); bell favours low rumbles.

    Patient course. There is no ship hull to lose. Wrong calls at the bedside or on Rounds push Mr. Reyes's care off course, and the ending shows what happens to him.

    Reality checks. Some Codex cards carry an amber note where the game bends physics or biology for playability, so you know exactly what is simplified.

    Scanning (E) files a layered Codex card. Hover the cursor over a red cell, white cell or platelet to see its name; click it (or press E) for its Codex card plus details on that exact cell. With nothing under the cursor, E scans the wall you are looking at: the endothelium, an artery's media, a valve cusp, myelin, a node of Ranvier, the lymph node's reticular meshwork — every surface is labelled on hover and opens its own Codex card and 3D model on click. Steer by click-dragging the view, right-dragging, or with the arrow keys.

    Valve study (Y). Near any valve, press Y to stop and orbit it. The heartbeat runs in slow motion; drag to look around, O flips to the other side (from the ventricle you see the chordae and papillary muscles), and every labelled part explains itself when clicked.

    Nervous system (K in the aortic arch). Take the carotid to a brain capillary, press K again to cross the blood-brain barrier into the motor cortex, then follow a pyramidal cell's axon: microtubule tracks, nodes of Ranvier and action potentials that overtake you, the pyramidal decussation, the terminal bouton and synaptic cleft, the lower motor neuron's cell body, the peripheral nerve with its Schwann cells, and the neuromuscular junction in the thigh, which drops you back into the muscle capillary.

    Lymph detour (K). In the muscle capillary, plasma leaks out between the endothelial cells. Press K to go with it: drift through the tissue, slip into a blind-ended lymphatic capillary, ride the pumping collecting lymphatics (their valves open only when the segment behind you squeezes), cross a lymph node with its follicles and macrophages, then take the cisterna chyli and thoracic duct back into the blood. In the tissue space, K takes you back into the capillary instead.

    Systems (Tab). Pick any system and learn it on its own: circulation, lymphatics, nervous system, and the GI, pulmonary, renal, hepatobiliary, pancreatic and pituitary worlds as they land. The body map in the corner redraws for wherever you are — the whole body for the circulation and lymph, a sagittal brain with the motor cortex, internal capsule and pyramids for the cerebral route, a cord cross-section at the anterior horn, the thigh for the femoral nerve.

    Study (T). Spaced-repetition flashcards written from what you have actually explored: recall each Codex entry, name a cell from its 3D model, say what comes next downstream, and answer the "predict before you see it" questions that pop up as you approach valves, the capillary, the blood-brain barrier and the decussation. Grade yourself; missed cards return in 10 minutes, known cards stretch out over days (saved in this browser). "Fly there" takes you back to look again.

    Autopilot (F) follows the vessel centreline and waits for valves. Scale is compressed for play: real capillaries are ~8 µm wide.
